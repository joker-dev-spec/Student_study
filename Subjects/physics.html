📐 Vectors

Definition

A vector is a physical quantity that has both magnitude and direction. Examples include displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, momentum and weight. A scalar has magnitude only — e.g. distance, speed, mass, time, temperature, energy and work.

Key Concepts

  • Resultant: a single vector that has the same effect as two or more vectors combined.
  • Components: a vector can be split into horizontal (x) and vertical (y) parts.
  • Triangle of forces: three forces in equilibrium are represented by the sides of a triangle taken in order.
  • Parallelogram law: if two vectors are represented by adjacent sides of a parallelogram, the resultant is the diagonal.
Resultant (perpendicular vectors) R = √(A² + B²)
Components Fx = F cos θ , Fy = F sin θ
Diagram Description Imagine two arrows drawn from a common point O: one arrow 3 units long pointing East, another 4 units long pointing North. Completing the parallelogram, the diagonal from O represents the resultant. Its length (5 units) gives the magnitude, and its angle with the horizontal (≈53°) gives the direction.
Worked Example A force of 6 N acts due East and a force of 8 N acts due North. Find the magnitude of the resultant.
R = √(6² + 8²) = √(36 + 64) = √100 = 10 N
JAMB / WAEC Tip Always resolve vectors into perpendicular components when three or more forces act. Remember: a vector at 90° to another has zero component along it. In equilibrium questions, resolve forces horizontally and vertically, then set each sum to zero.

⚖️ Equilibrium

Definition

A body is in equilibrium when the net force acting on it is zero and the net turning effect (moment) about any point is zero. The body is either at rest (static equilibrium) or moving with a constant velocity (dynamic equilibrium).

Conditions for Equilibrium

  • Sum of all forces in any direction = 0 (ΣF = 0)
  • Sum of clockwise moments = sum of anticlockwise moments (ΣM = 0)
Principle of Moments Clockwise moment = Anticlockwise moment
Moment of a Force Moment = Force × perpendicular distance from pivot
Diagram Description A seesaw balanced on a fulcrum. A heavy child sits close to the pivot; a lighter child sits farther away. The clockwise turning effect of one equals the anticlockwise turning effect of the other, so the beam stays horizontal.
Worked Example A uniform metre rule of weight 1.5 N is pivoted at the 40 cm mark. A weight of 2.5 N is hung at the 10 cm mark. What weight must be hung at the 80 cm mark to balance it?
Weight of rule acts at 50 cm (10 cm right of pivot): clockwise moment = 1.5 × 10 = 15 N·cm
2.5 N at 10 cm (30 cm left of pivot): anticlockwise = 2.5 × 30 = 75 N·cm
Extra clockwise needed = 75 − 15 = 60 N·cm
W × (80 − 40) = 60 → W = 1.5 N
JAMB / WAEC Tip For a uniform rule, always take the weight of the rule as acting at its geometric centre (50 cm). Choose the pivot as your reference point so that the moment of the pivot force is zero. This simplifies calculations.

🚀 Motion

Definition

Motion is a change in position of a body with time relative to a reference point. It is described using distance, displacement, speed, velocity and acceleration.

  • Speed = distance / time (scalar)
  • Velocity = displacement / time (vector)
  • Acceleration = change in velocity / time (vector)
Equations of Motion (uniform acceleration) v = u + at  |  s = ut + ½at²  |  v² = u² + 2as
Distance in the nth second Sₙ = u + a(2n − 1)/2
Diagram Description A velocity–time graph: a straight sloping line starting at height u on the y-axis and rising to v. The gradient of the line equals acceleration; the area under the line equals the distance travelled. A horizontal line means constant velocity (zero acceleration).
Worked Example A stone is thrown vertically upward with a velocity of 20 m/s (g = 10 m/s²). Find the maximum height reached.
At maximum height, v = 0.
v² = u² − 2gs → 0 = 20² − 2(10)s
s = 400 / 20 = 20 m
JAMB / WAEC Tip For bodies thrown upwards, take g as negative (−) since it opposes motion. At the highest point the velocity is momentarily zero but acceleration is still g downward. The area under a velocity–time graph gives distance; the area under an acceleration–time graph gives velocity change.

💡 Light

Definition

Light is a form of electromagnetic radiation that travels in straight lines (rectilinear propagation) and can be reflected, refracted or dispersed. Reflection is the bouncing of light off a surface; refraction is the bending of light as it moves between media of different densities.

Laws of Reflection

  • The incident ray, reflected ray and normal all lie in the same plane.
  • The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection (i = r).

Laws of Refraction (Snell's Law)

Snell's Law n = sin i / sin r
Refractive Index & Critical Angle n = c / v  |  sin C = 1 / n
Diagram Description A ray of light passing from air into a glass block bends towards the normal at the point of entry because glass is denser. On leaving the glass, it bends away from the normal. At the critical angle, the refracted ray travels along the surface; beyond it, total internal reflection occurs.
Worked Example The refractive index of glass is 1.5. Find the speed of light in glass (c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s).
n = c / v → v = c / n = (3 × 10⁸) / 1.5 = 2 × 10⁸ m/s
JAMB / WAEC Tip Light bends towards the normal when entering a denser medium and away from the normal when entering a less dense medium. Total internal reflection only occurs when light travels from a denser to a less dense medium and the angle of incidence exceeds the critical angle.

🌊 Waves

Definition

A wave is a disturbance that transfers energy through a medium without transferring matter. Waves are classified as transverse (particles vibrate perpendicular to wave direction — e.g. light, water waves) or longitudinal (particles vibrate parallel to wave direction — e.g. sound).

Wave Equation v = f λ   (speed = frequency × wavelength)
Period and Frequency T = 1 / f
Diagram Description A sine curve showing a transverse wave. The wavelength (λ) is the distance between two consecutive crests (or troughs). The amplitude (A) is the maximum displacement from the rest position. The frequency (f) is the number of complete waves passing a point per second.

Wave Phenomena

  • Reflection — bouncing of waves off a barrier.
  • Refraction — change in speed and direction when entering a new medium.
  • Diffraction — spreading/bending of waves around obstacles or through gaps.
  • Interference — superposition of two waves (constructive or destructive).
Worked Example A wave has a frequency of 200 Hz and a wavelength of 2 m. Find its speed.
v = f λ = 200 × 2 = 400 m/s
JAMB / WAEC Tip Sound cannot travel through a vacuum (it needs a medium) but light can. In constructive interference, amplitudes add; in destructive interference, they subtract. Diffraction is more noticeable when the gap size is comparable to the wavelength.
