🔵 Particulate Nature of Matter

Definition

Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space. It is made up of tiny particles — atoms, molecules and ions. The kinetic molecular theory explains the behaviour of matter.

Postulates of the Kinetic Molecular Theory

  • All matter is made of tiny particles.
  • Particles are in constant, random motion.
  • Particles have kinetic energy which increases with temperature.
  • There are forces of attraction between particles, strongest in solids and weakest in gases.
  • Particles have negligible volume compared to the space between them.

States of Matter

  • Solid: fixed shape and volume; particles closely packed in a regular pattern, vibrating about fixed positions.
  • Liquid: fixed volume, no fixed shape; particles close but able to slide past one another.
  • Gas: no fixed shape or volume; particles far apart, moving rapidly and randomly.

Changes of State

Melting (solid→liquid), freezing (liquid→solid), evaporation (liquid→gas), condensation (gas→liquid), sublimation (solid→gas directly, e.g. iodine, dry ice).

Diagram Description Three boxes side by side. In the solid box, particles are tightly packed in neat rows. In the liquid box, particles are close but irregularly arranged. In the gas box, particles are widely spread with lots of empty space between them.
Worked Example Explain why a gas fills its container completely.
Gas particles are in constant rapid random motion with very weak intermolecular forces and large spaces between them. They move freely in all directions and spread out to occupy the entire volume of the container.
JAMB / WAEC Tip Diffusion rate increases with temperature and decreases with increasing molar mass. According to Graham's Law, lighter gases diffuse faster: Rate₁/Rate₂ = √(M₂/M₁). Remember hydrogen is the lightest and diffuses fastest.

⚛️ Periodic Table

Definition

The periodic table arranges elements in order of increasing atomic number. It has 7 periods (horizontal rows) and 18 groups (vertical columns). Elements in the same group have the same number of valence electrons and similar chemical properties.

Key Groups

  • Group 1 — Alkali metals: Li, Na, K... soft, highly reactive, 1 valence electron.
  • Group 2 — Alkaline earth metals: Be, Mg, Ca... reactive, 2 valence electrons.
  • Group 7 — Halogens: F, Cl, Br, I... very reactive non-metals, 7 valence electrons.
  • Group 8 (18) — Noble gases: He, Ne, Ar... inert, full outer shells.
  • Transition metals: Groups 3–12, e.g. Fe, Cu, Zn.

Periodic Trends

  • Atomic radius decreases across a period, increases down a group.
  • Electronegativity increases across a period, decreases down a group.
  • Ionization energy increases across a period, decreases down a group.
Diagram Description A grid shaped like a wide staircase. Metals occupy the left and centre; non-metals the upper right. A zig-zag line separates metals from non-metals, with metalloids (Si, Ge) along the border. Noble gases form the far-right column.
Worked Example Why does sodium (Group 1) react vigorously with water while chlorine (Group 7) does not?
Sodium has a single valence electron which it readily loses to form Na⁺ (strong reducing agent), so it reacts vigorously with water. Chlorine needs to gain an electron and does not donate readily, so it does not react with water the same way.
JAMB / WAEC Tip Fluorine is the most electronegative element. Across a period, atomic radius decreases because the increasing nuclear charge pulls electrons closer. Down a group, radius increases due to added shells. Know these trends — they appear every year.

🔗 Chemical Bonding

Definition

Chemical bonding is the force of attraction that holds atoms together in a compound. Atoms bond to achieve a stable (noble gas) electron configuration.

Types of Bonding

  • Ionic (electrovalent) bonding: transfer of electrons from a metal to a non-metal, forming oppositely charged ions. E.g. NaCl (Na⁺Cl⁻).
  • Covalent bonding: sharing of electron pairs between non-metal atoms. E.g. H₂, Cl₂, H₂O, CH₄.
  • Metallic bonding: electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions and a sea of delocalised electrons.
Covalent bond types Single (H–H) · Double (O=O) · Triple (N≡N)
Diagram Description For NaCl: sodium atom donates its single outer electron to chlorine, forming Na⁺ and Cl⁻ which attract electrostatically. For CH₄: carbon shares its four outer electrons with four hydrogen atoms, forming four single covalent bonds arranged tetrahedrally (109.5°).
Worked Example Explain why NaCl conducts electricity when molten but not when solid.
In solid NaCl, ions are locked in a fixed lattice and cannot move. When molten, the lattice breaks down and the Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions become free to move, carrying charge through the liquid — so it conducts.
JAMB / WAEC Tip Ionic compounds: high melting points, soluble in water, conduct when molten/aqueous. Covalent compounds: low melting points, often insoluble, do not conduct. Use VSEPR theory for shapes: 4 bonding pairs = tetrahedral, 3 = trigonal planar, 2 = linear.

🧫 Acids and Bases

Definition

An acid is a substance that produces hydrogen ions (H⁺) when dissolved in water, or a proton donor. A base is a proton acceptor; a soluble base is called an alkali and produces OH⁻ ions in water.

Properties

  • Acids turn blue litmus paper red; bases turn red litmus paper blue.
  • Acids react with metals to release hydrogen gas.
  • Acids react with bases to form salt and water (neutralisation).
  • Strong acids (HCl, HNO₃, H₂SO₄) ionize completely; weak acids (CH₃COOH) ionize partially.
pH and pOH pH = −log[H⁺]  |  pOH = −log[OH⁻]  |  pH + pOH = 14
Neutralisation Acid + Base → Salt + Water
Diagram Description A pH colour scale from 0 (deep red, strongly acidic) through 7 (green, neutral) to 14 (deep purple, strongly alkaline). Universal indicator is used to determine the pH value; litmus only distinguishes acid from base.
Worked Example Calculate the pH of a 0.001 M HCl solution.
HCl is a strong acid → [H⁺] = 0.001 = 10⁻³ M
pH = −log(10⁻³) = 3
JAMB / WAEC Tip Acid salt (e.g. NaHSO₄) forms from partial neutralisation; normal salt (e.g. Na₂SO₄) from complete neutralisation. A base that dissolves in water is an alkali. Remember: pH < 7 acidic, = 7 neutral, > 7 alkaline.

🧬 Organic Chemistry Introduction

Definition

Organic chemistry is the study of carbon compounds. Carbon forms four covalent bonds and can bond with itself to form long chains and rings — a property called catenation. Hydrocarbons contain only carbon and hydrogen.

Homologous Series

  • Alkanes: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ — saturated (single bonds). E.g. methane CH₄, ethane C₂H₆.
  • Alkenes: CₙH₂ₙ — unsaturated with a C=C double bond. E.g. ethene C₂H₄.
  • Alkynes: CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ — unsaturated with a C≡C triple bond. E.g. ethyne C₂H₂.
  • Alkanols (alcohols): contain the –OH group. E.g. ethanol C₂H₅OH.
  • Alkanoic acids: contain the –COOH group. E.g. ethanoic acid CH₃COOH.
General Formulae Alkane CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ · Alkene CₙH₂ₙ · Alkyne CₙH₂ₙ₋₂
Diagram Description Methane: a central carbon atom bonded to four hydrogen atoms in a tetrahedral shape. Ethene: two carbon atoms joined by a double bond, each carbon also bonded to two hydrogens, all atoms in a flat plane.
Worked Example Name the compound CH₃CH₂CH₂OH using IUPAC rules.
Count carbons: 3 → prefix "prop-".
Functional group –OH on the first carbon → suffix "-1-ol".
Name: Propan-1-ol
JAMB / WAEC Tip Alkanes undergo substitution; alkenes and alkynes undergo addition reactions (they decolourise bromine water — a test for unsaturation). Isomers have the same molecular formula but different structural formulae.
