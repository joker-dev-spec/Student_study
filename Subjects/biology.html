🔬 The Cell

Definition

The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of all living organisms. Cells were first observed by Robert Hooke in 1665. The cell theory states that all living things are made of cells, and all cells come from pre-existing cells.

Cell Organelles and Functions

  • Cell membrane: selectively permeable; controls movement of substances in and out of the cell.
  • Nucleus: contains DNA/chromosomes; controls all cell activities.
  • Mitochondrion: site of aerobic respiration; releases energy (ATP). "Powerhouse of the cell."
  • Ribosome: site of protein synthesis.
  • Chloroplast: contains chlorophyll; site of photosynthesis (plant cells only).
  • Vacuole: stores water, salts and food. Large central vacuole in plant cells.
  • Cell wall: rigid, made of cellulose; provides support (plant cells only).
  • Cytoplasm: jelly-like medium where cell reactions occur.
Diagram Description A plant cell drawn as a rectangular box with a thick outer cell wall and inner cell membrane. Inside, a large central vacuole dominates, with a nucleus pushed to the side and green chloroplasts scattered through the cytoplasm. An animal cell is rounder, lacks a cell wall and chloroplasts, and has many small vacuoles instead of one large one.

Movement In and Out of Cells

  • Diffusion: movement of particles from high to low concentration.
  • Osmosis: movement of water through a semi-permeable membrane from high to low water potential.
  • Active transport: movement against a concentration gradient using energy (ATP).
Worked Example A cell with 46 chromosomes undergoes mitosis. How many chromosomes will each daughter cell have?
Mitosis produces two genetically identical cells with the same chromosome number as the parent: 46 chromosomes each. (Meiosis would halve it to 23.)
JAMB / WAEC Tip Know the differences between plant and animal cells: plant cells have a cell wall, chloroplasts, and a large central vacuole; animal cells have centrioles and many small vacuoles. Mitochondria are present in BOTH.

🧩 Tissues

Definition

A tissue is a group of similar cells that are adapted (specialised) to perform a particular function. In complex organisms, tissues combine to form organs and organ systems — this is called levels of organisation.

Animal Tissues

  • Epithelial tissue: covers body surfaces and lines cavities; protects and aids absorption/secretion.
  • Connective tissue: connects and supports organs. Includes blood, bone, cartilage and adipose tissue.
  • Muscle tissue: contracts to produce movement. Types: skeletal (voluntary), smooth (involuntary), cardiac.
  • Nervous tissue: transmits electrical impulses; made of neurons.

Plant Tissues

  • Xylem: transports water and minerals from roots upward.
  • Phloem: transports manufactured food (sugars) from leaves to other parts.
  • Epidermis: protective outer layer.
  • Cambium: produces new xylem and phloem (secondary growth).
Diagram Description A cross-section of a plant stem showing an outer epidermis, then a ring of phloem on the outside, a thin cambium layer, and xylem toward the centre. Arrows show water moving up through the xylem and sugars moving down (and up) through the phloem.
Worked Example A plant is ringed (bark removed all around the stem). Explain what happens.
The ring removes the phloem, which transports sugars. Water transport (xylem) still works, but food cannot be transported to the roots. The roots eventually starve and the plant dies. This demonstrates phloem's role.
JAMB / WAEC Tip Remember the order of organisation: Cell → Tissue → Organ → System → Organism. Blood is the classic example of a connective tissue. Xylem = water up; Phloem = food (both up and down).

🌿 Photosynthesis

Definition

Photosynthesis is the process by which green plants manufacture their own food (glucose) from carbon dioxide and water, using light energy trapped by chlorophyll. It occurs in the chloroplasts of plant cells.

Equation 6CO₂ + 6H₂O —(light, chlorophyll)→ C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

Requirements

  • Carbon dioxide — from the air through stomata.
  • Water — absorbed by roots, transported by xylem.
  • Light energy — absorbed by chlorophyll in chloroplasts.
  • Chlorophyll — green pigment that traps light.

Two Stages

  • Light-dependent stage: occurs on thylakoid membranes; light splits water (photolysis) releasing oxygen and producing ATP.
  • Light-independent stage (Calvin cycle): occurs in the stroma; CO₂ is fixed to form glucose.
Diagram Description A leaf with sunlight arrows hitting it. Through the underside, CO₂ enters through small openings called stomata. From the stem, water travels up in the xylem. Arrows point out showing oxygen released and glucose formed. Inside a leaf cell, the chloroplast is magnified to show green chlorophyll.
Worked Example A plant is placed in the dark for 48 hours. What happens to starch in its leaves, and how would you test?
Without light, no photosynthesis occurs, so no new starch is made and existing starch is used up. If you decolourise a leaf with hot alcohol and add iodine solution, it turns brown (stays brown) instead of blue-black — proving no starch is present.
JAMB / WAEC Tip Photosynthesis is the reverse of respiration. Test for starch uses iodine solution (blue-black = starch present). A plant with a variegated leaf shows that only green (chlorophyll-containing) parts produce starch — proving chlorophyll is necessary.

🫁 Respiratory System

Definition

The respiratory system is responsible for gaseous exchange — taking in oxygen for respiration and removing carbon dioxide. In humans it consists of the nose, trachea, bronchi, bronchioles and lungs (alveoli).

Parts and Functions

  • Nose: filters, warms and moistens incoming air.
  • Trachea (windpipe): carries air to the lungs; lined with cartilage rings and cilia.
  • Bronchi: two tubes leading into each lung.
  • Bronchioles: smaller branches of the bronchi.
  • Alveoli: tiny air sacs where gas exchange occurs.
  • Diaphragm and ribs: cause pressure changes for breathing.

Mechanism of Breathing

  • Inhalation: diaphragm contracts and flattens; ribs move up and out; thoracic volume increases; pressure decreases; air rushes in.
  • Exhalation: diaphragm relaxes and domes upward; ribs move down and in; volume decreases; pressure increases; air is pushed out.
Diagram Description A pair of lungs with the trachea splitting into two bronchi. Each bronchus branches into bronchioles ending in clusters of tiny balloon-like alveoli. Around each alveolus is a network of capillaries. A magnified inset shows oxygen diffusing from the alveolus into the blood and carbon dioxide diffusing out.
Worked Example Explain two adaptations of the alveoli for efficient gas exchange.
1. Large surface area — millions of alveoli provide a huge area for diffusion.
2. Thin walls (one cell thick) and rich blood supply — short diffusion distance and a maintained concentration gradient.
JAMB / WAEC Tip Aerobic respiration: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy (in mitochondria). Anaerobic respiration in muscles produces lactic acid (causes cramps); in yeast it produces ethanol and CO₂. Remember — breathing is mechanical, respiration is chemical.

🌍 Ecology

Definition

Ecology is the study of the relationship between living organisms and their environment. An ecosystem consists of the biotic (living) community and the abiotic (non-living) factors interacting together.

Key Terms

  • Population: all members of one species in an area.
  • Community: all populations living together in an area.
  • Habitat: the place where an organism lives.
  • Niche: the role an organism plays in its ecosystem.
  • Carrying capacity: maximum population an environment can support.

Feeding Relationships

  • Producers (autotrophs): make their own food — green plants.
  • Consumers: primary (herbivores), secondary and tertiary (carnivores).
  • Decomposers: break down dead matter — bacteria and fungi.
Diagram Description A food chain drawn as arrows: Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Hawk. Each arrow points in the direction of energy flow. Beside it, a pyramid of numbers shows a wide base of producers narrowing to few top predators, illustrating energy loss at each trophic level.
Worked Example In the food chain Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake, what is the role of the frog?
The frog eats the grasshopper (a primary consumer), so it is a secondary consumer (second-order consumer). It is carnivorous at this level.
JAMB / WAEC Tip Only about 10% of energy passes from one trophic level to the next — energy is lost as heat, in movement and in waste. The nitrogen cycle involves nitrogen fixation (N₂→NH₃), nitrification (NH₃→nitrates) and denitrification. Density-dependent factors (disease) depend on population size; density-independent factors (floods) do not.
